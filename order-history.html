Overview

What if customers could simply explain what they needed?

Order History is a key part of the adidas post-purchase experience, where customers can track deliveries, manage returns, check refunds, and resolve issues after making a purchase.

The project explored a broader vision for post-purchase support: what if customers could simply explain what they needed and the system could connect that need with the right information and action?

AI was part of that design vision, acting as a layer between customer intent and the existing post-sales ecosystem.

However, delivering that full vision required capabilities and integrations that were beyond the scope of a first release.

So we deliberately defined an MVP focused on building the foundation first: a modular Order History experience that made order status, products, deliveries, and post-sales actions easier to understand and use.

The MVP initially launched within Returns & Refunds and reduced post-sales Customer Service contacts by 8–10%.

The AI-assisted interactions shown in this case study were part of the broader experience I designed, but were not included in the MVP.

Problem

Order History received more than 7 million visits, yet 64% of visitors still contacted Customer Service after using it.

The problem was not simply a lack of information.

Most of what customers needed already existed, but it was distributed across different pages, services, and flows.

Customers had to understand what an order status meant, find the right service, and decide which action applied to their situation.

Even a simple question such as “Where is my order?” could require navigating through several parts of the experience.

In practice, the product was asking customers to understand how adidas had organised its post-sales services before they could solve their problem.

Customers arrived with a need. The experience expected them to translate that need into the right feature or flow.

Understanding why

Before redesigning the experience, I wanted to understand why customers came to Order History and what prevented them from resolving their needs independently.

I led a qualitative study focused on user behaviour, not just the visual interface.

18 participants took part in unmoderated desktop usability tests across the US, UK, and Germany.

The research focused on four questions:

  • Do customers understand the information being shown?
  • Can they discover the available post-sales actions?
  • Can they resolve issues without contacting Customer Service?
  • When and why do they access Order History?

Most participants had no difficulty finding their orders within My Account. The problems started once they entered Order History.

Customers struggled to identify which post-sales actions were available, the information hierarchy did not support consistent scanning, and the page structure did not reflect what they were actually paying attention to.

For almost every participant, the expected delivery date was the most important piece of information. What they needed next depended heavily on the state of the order.

When an order was still in progress, customers were mainly looking for actions. Once an order was complete, they were more often looking for information.

This led to an important insight: Order History was not simply a list of past purchases. Its purpose changed depending on what was happening with each order.

The returns flow reinforced this learning. Most participants went directly to Return or Exchange rather than exploring the order details first.

When the right action was visible, customers knew what they wanted to do. The challenge was making the right information and actions visible at the right moment.

Opportunity

The immediate opportunity was to transform Order History from a passive purchase history into an active post-sales service.

Customers should be able to quickly answer three questions:

  • What is happening?
  • What does it affect?
  • What can I do about it?

But the research also revealed a broader opportunity.

Even if all the right information and actions were visible, customers still had to translate their real-world problem into the way adidas had organised its services.

A customer does not think: I need to use shipment tracking. They think: My shoes were supposed to arrive yesterday. Or: These are too small. Or: Where is my refund?

That led to a broader design question: what if customers could start by explaining their problem instead of having to find the right feature first?

Product vision

I designed Order History around two connected ideas.

The first was a modular product system that could adapt to different order states and surface the most relevant information and actions at the right moment.

The second was an intent-based layer that could understand what the customer was trying to resolve and connect them with the right part of the system.

This is where AI made sense, because natural language could become a new way to access the existing post-sales ecosystem.

The customer could describe what was happening. The system could identify the relevant order, retrieve the necessary information, and surface the appropriate action.

The existing tracking, returns, refunds, and order-management systems would continue to be the source of truth. AI would connect customer intent with those services.

Building the foundation

Three zones, one card

The existing Order History experience treated orders mainly as a historical record. I reorganised the experience around what customers might need after making a purchase.

Each order brought together:

  • current status
  • delivery information
  • products and quantities
  • relevant post-sales actions

The challenge was supporting many different states without designing a separate experience for every scenario.

This led to a modular card system. Each card was organised around three questions.

What is happening? Status, dates, and delivery estimates should be understandable at a glance.

What does it affect? Products, sizes, and quantities should remain visible and consistent throughout the experience.

What can I do about it? Actions should respond to the current order state rather than remain generic.

This structure became the foundation for deliveries, returns, refunds, and other post-sales scenarios.

Designing around intent

How can we help with your order?

As part of the same vision, once the system was structured around customer needs, I explored how customers could access it without having to manually navigate the entire experience.

The interaction could begin with a simple question: How can we help with your order?

From the customer’s response, the system would follow four steps.

  • Understand: Interpret what the customer is trying to resolve.
  • Identify: Determine which order, delivery, or product is related to the request.
  • Check: Retrieve the necessary order information, policies, and available services.
  • Present: Show the most relevant information or next action using the existing product interface.

The key was for AI to sit between customer intent and the existing capabilities.

It would interpret the request, but not make business decisions. Return eligibility, refund amounts, cancellations, and delivery promises would come from the systems of record. Higher-impact actions would require customer confirmation, and unresolved cases would be escalated to Customer Service with the relevant context attached.

Defining the MVP

The MVP decision did not change the product vision; it changed the order in which we decided to build it.

The full experience depended on several capabilities working together: intent recognition, live order context, business rules, service integrations, and safeguards around transactional actions.

Trying to implement all of this in the first release would have significantly increased the complexity and risk of the project.

So we deliberately reduced the scope.

The MVP focused on the foundation: a modular Order History experience, initially applied to Returns & Refunds.

Returns & Refunds provided a strong test for the system because it contained some of the most complex post-sales states and actions. If the architecture worked there, it could later scale to other scenarios.

The MVP established:

  • reusable order components
  • a clearer information hierarchy
  • contextual actions
  • consistent product information
  • a scalable structure for adding new post-sales services

This allowed us to improve the existing experience immediately while building a foundation that could support a broader evolution of the product.

Results

The MVP produced measurable impact.

8–10%
Reduction in post-sales Customer Service contacts after launch

This result validated an important part of the hypothesis: bringing relevant information and actions together in a clearer, more contextual experience could reduce the effort customers needed to make after a purchase.

The result does not validate the AI layer, because that part of the experience was not included in the released MVP.

How I would measure the full experience

Because getting an answer is not the same as solving the problem.

The reduction in Customer Service contacts was an important metric for the first release.

For the broader experience, I would focus more directly on whether customers were actually able to resolve what they came to do.

The primary metric would be resolution rate: how many customer needs are successfully resolved without requiring another channel?

I would also measure:

  • time to resolution
  • escalation rate
  • failed actions
  • satisfaction after completing the task
What changed

The MVP transformed Order History from a passive record into a more useful post-purchase tool.

But the design vision took the idea one step further.

What if customers did not even need to understand how Order History was organised?

Instead of finding the right service, they could explain what they needed. The system could connect that intent with real order data, business rules, and existing product actions.

The AI-assisted experience was part of that product vision. The MVP deliberately stopped one layer earlier, establishing the architecture needed to make that vision possible.
